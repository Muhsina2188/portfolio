The opportunity

Search beyond keywords

Alamy's customers found visual assets through keyword search. Smart Search helped improve keyword queries, but there was no conversational way to describe the image you wanted, and no way to search by voice. "A candid woman laughing outdoors in warm golden light" is a sentence, not a list of keywords.

As conversational AI became more familiar, a Search Assistant could make discovery more natural: describe what you need in your own words, refine it through conversation, and explore relevant images in the same place. Better discovery could support the path to choosing and buying an image, though the Assistant's commercial impact still has to be shown by its own metrics.

The product-design challenge

Not just adding a chatbot

The job was to design a genuinely useful conversational image search, and fit it into an established website with existing search behaviours, controls and commercial priorities.

How do we give Search Assistant a distinct role, a conversational route for describing and refining what you want, alongside keyword search and Smart Search, without forcing anyone away from a familiar workflow?

Research

Starting with the problem, not the chatbot

Before designing anything, I framed what we were solving and what would have to be true for a conversational assistant to be worth building.

Problem

Semantic search improved result quality, but people still had to squeeze their project needs into a search box. With no conversational interface, there was no way to explore, refine and discuss a brief.

Hypothesis

A conversational search experience would let people discuss briefs, refine ideas and see results inline, turning search into a collaborative assistant rather than a static tool.

User goal

Find the right images or videos quickly and confidently through natural conversation, without having to search, filter or contact support.

Business goal

Help customers discover and buy through conversation, understand that this kind of search exists, and reduce friction and reliance on support.

Questions I set out to answer

  • Is there a real desire for a conversational search experience?
  • What language do our customers naturally use when they describe an image?
  • How will people know the Assistant exists, and that they can search with a full brief?
  • Should it sit alongside classic search so people can use either?
  • How should the results change as the conversation refines them?

Competitor analysis: one brief, five search engines

I gave the same long, natural-language brief to five competitors: two people in their 50s walking hand in hand along a riverside in spring, in the 1940s, with period clothing, dilapidated buildings and horses in the field.

Even where competitors accepted a conversational request, nothing told people they could search that way. That became a design requirement: the Assistant had to announce what it could do.

Best practices I designed against

  • Set expectations at the start. Introduce what the AI can and can't do, and help people write good prompts. This became the first-use guidance.
  • Fit the product, but stand apart. The chat should match the product's design while being clearly distinct as a conversation. This became the AI visual pattern.
  • Build trust through transparency. Show where answers come from and handle errors honestly. This became the image references in every answer.
Designing the Search Assistant experience

Conversation and images, side by side

The core experience had to combine the flexibility of conversation with the practical goal of finding visual assets. People should be able to describe what they need, refine it, and still see the images being discussed.

How the experience evolved

The layout went through several directions before conversation and results settled side by side.

I designed a split layout with the conversation on the left and image results on the right, so people can keep talking without losing sight of what they're exploring. I also designed how images referenced in the conversation are highlighted.

Designing for mobile

The same behaviours, top to bottom

The desktop side-by-side layout wouldn't translate to a narrow screen. On mobile, I designed a vertically arranged, slide-down experience with the conversation and results organised top to bottom, keeping the essential conversation and image-discovery behaviours on a smaller screen.

Integrating the experience

Testing exposed a placement problem

With the experience designed, I worked out how to bring it into Alamy's existing search journey. In UserTesting.com sessions, using working prototypes deployed to Vercel rather than clickable mock-ups, I asked participants to find Search Assistant, describe their experience, rate its ease of use and suggest improvements.

User testing Participants initially struggled to notice the Assistant, and had difficulty telling it apart from Smart Search.

I explored more than six entry points, including placing it within filters, a toggle between conventional and AI search, and a dedicated Search Assistant page linked from search results. Three of them:

Evidence
Moving the Assistant around didn't solve it. Even when it was visually separated, people overlooked it or confused it with Smart Search.
Interpretation
Separation alone wasn't enough. The Assistant needed a clear identity and a discoverable place in the existing workflow, and Smart Search needed to stop competing with it.
Decision
Simplify Smart Search into a compact toggle, validated by an A/B test, and give the Assistant its own row and visual identity.
Trade-off
Changing a control that already contributed to conversions, which is why it was tested before it was confirmed.

The released entry point

Its own row, its own identity

In the released design, Smart Search stays a compact toggle in the main search bar. Search Assistant gets its own row, a distinct purple-gradient button, a sparkle icon, and the copy "Describe the image or video you're looking for." The new capability looks different, while familiar search controls and navigation stay where they were.

Design-system considerations

Alamy's green already meant "main action", and the only purple in the design system was an icon token, never meant for a button people press again and again. Rather than borrow it, I created a dedicated AI button: its own colour tokens, plus pill and floating versions with hover and disabled states. I built it in code myself, handed the code to engineering, and added it to Alamy's design system as a new style of the existing Button. It behaves like every other button on the site, and future AI features can reuse it instead of reinventing it.

Beyond search results

I later explored entry points from the homepage and product pages, where the button floats and expands with a hint as people scroll.

Picking up where classic search left off

Opening the Assistant doesn't mean starting over. When someone opens it after a keyword search, it carries that search into the conversation ("Since you were searching for 'cats', let me pull that up for you. I can help refine the results too.") and shows matching results straight away, ready to refine.

Collaboration and feasibility

What was mine, and what was the team's

  • Mine: the Search Assistant experience on desktop and mobile, how it integrates into Alamy.com, the entry-point exploration, the Smart Search simplification, the AI button (designed, coded and added to the design system), and user testing.
  • With the PM: releasing as a beta, and iterating from customer feedback.
  • With engineering: I built the AI button in code and gave them the code to integrate, so what shipped matches the design exactly.
  • With analytics: the A/B test of the Smart Search redesign, and tracking for the beta.

How I worked: production-ready prototypes, not Figma click-throughs

Instead of building every step in Figma and wiring it into a prototype, I built coded, production-ready prototypes with Claude Code and Alamy's code-connected design system, and shared those with stakeholders. People reviewed the real experience, with real components and real behaviour, so feedback was about the product rather than about a mock-up.

Figma: ideation and source of truth

Where new ideas start, and where every final design is documented, so the team always has one place to check what was agreed. Engineers built from these designs.

Code: prototypes stakeholders can use

Production-ready prototypes for reviews and decisions. Engineers also used them alongside the Figma designs, to see exactly how the experience should behave.

It made testing easier and earlier, too. I deployed the prototypes to Vercel and shared the links in UserTesting.com sessions, so participants used a working experience in their own browser rather than a clickable Figma mock-up. It behaved like the real product, so their reactions were closer to how they'd use the live site, and I could test earlier, as soon as a version was built.

Skipping the step-by-step Figma prototype is part of how the project went from concept to live beta in under four months. I set the direction and made the design decisions. AI sped up the build.

Live beta, learning and continuous improvement

Release when it's good to go, then keep improving

Search Assistant went live as a beta in September 2026, less than four months after the project began. Our approach is to release when the experience is good to go, learn from real customer feedback and usage, and improve over time, rather than wait for every detail to be perfect. The longer-term goal is to evolve the beta into the full version.

Next: shorter answers with a few best-fit picks

Experiment One current area of exploration is the response style. In the live beta, answers run as long paragraphs with raw reference codes inline, which makes them hard to scan. Instead of returning extensive text and a broad set of results, the Assistant may summarise what it found, recommend three best-fit images directly in the conversation, each with a short reason, and suggest ways to narrow the search.

This response style is still being explored and tested. It isn't released.

What I learned

  • Design the experience, then earn its place. A good conversational experience still fails if people can't find it or can't tell it apart from what's already there.
  • Separation isn't identity. Moving the Assistant away from Smart Search didn't make it noticeable. A clear identity and place in the hierarchy did the work that position couldn't.
  • Validate changes to what already works. Smart Search contributed to conversions, so simplifying it was tested before it was confirmed.

The problem and stakes

One feature, doing six different jobs

Should one overloaded feature keep serving customers and internal curators, or do these audiences need distinct experiences?

Lightbox was built to let customers save images for later. Over a decade it quietly became the tool behind customer saves, sharing, internal curation, sales outreach, enterprise downloads and even export workflows. It was never designed to carry that weight, and the brief I was given was simply to rebuild it.

The evidence

Four sources, each with a blind spot

No single method could answer the question, so I combined four and was explicit about what each could and couldn't tell me.

The insight

The symptoms pointed to one cause

Dead endAnalytics

Almost everyone who opened an image inside a Lightbox only viewed it. Very few downloaded, added to cart or saved again. That turned "Lightbox feels like a dead end" from an anecdote into evidence, and set the bar for what "fixed" had to mean.

CurationAnalytics

An internal editorial series, "See The World In 40 Images", drew a large share of all Lightbox traffic from just a handful of editions. But each edition's page was reused once its run ended. The demand for curated content was real. It had nowhere permanent to live.

3/3Interviews, testing, competitive review

"No persistent navigation between collections" was the only problem confirmed by all three qualitative methods at once.

Together these showed that the usability problems were symptoms. The cause was one feature serving two audiences with very different needs: customers saving a handful of images, and professional curators managing briefs of 1,000+ assets.

The decision

Not "Lightbox 2.0", but two experiences on one foundation

Evidence
Customers and curators work at different scales with different goals, and five internal teams had built their own workflows on top of the same feature.
Interpretation
Rebuilding one unified tool would repeat the original mistake.
Decision
Separate Customer Collections and Curated Collections, sharing one foundation for data, permissions, sharing and restrictions.
Trade-off
Two experiences to design and maintain, and a migration question for existing Lightboxes, now open with the PM.

Why I started over

Not a copy of Customer Collections

After Customer Collections, it was tempting to reuse its journey structure. I didn't: curators do different work at a much larger scale, with denser information. So I started from the raw research and then stress-tested the requirements against it.

The requirements mostly held up. But three themes, export and API access, real-time collaboration, and territory-scoped views, had no coverage across all 40 requirements.

Two different interviewees raised them independently.

What I mapped

Four flows, evidence first, gaps included

Every step is traced to a source, as in Customer Collections. This map also says openly when a journey rests on requirements rather than a validated complaint.

The decision

Going back to the tool I'd already designed

Seven wireframes in, working from the requirements alone, I went back to the Curation Tool file. Curated collection screens had already been started there in Alamy DS 2.0, some marked "Ready for dev", and my new wireframes didn't match them.

Evidence
The real tool was built for an image-heavy, expert workflow in ways my wireframes had ignored.
Interpretation
My first version was reasonable guesswork, made without a view of the real tool.
Decision
Rebuild the wireframes to match the real tool, rather than defend the version I'd already invested in.
Trade-off
Seven screens of rework, in exchange for designs that fit how curators already work.
Next check
NextTest the rebuilt screens with the content team.

How I used AI here

I used Claude Code to stress-test the requirements against the research, and Figma MCP to work from the real Alamy DS 2.0 components. They helped me inspect and compare faster. The decision to rebuild, and every design choice in the rebuild, was mine.

What I designed

Matched to the real system

Four of the seven rebuilt screens. Each follows the real Curation Tool's patterns where I could check them. Where I couldn't, the screen says so.

Status and reflection

Six screens checked, three gaps handed to the PM, testing next

Six of seven wireframes are confirmed against the real Curation Tool. The three evidenced gaps, export and API access, real-time collaboration, and territory-scoped views, are flagged for the PM rather than designed around on my own.

NextUser testing with the content team. Until then, this design is not validated or ready for handoff.

What I'd take from this

The useful instinct wasn't building fast. It was being willing to throw out seven screens the moment better information appeared. The rebuilt wireframes are more accurate because the first version was wrong in specific, nameable ways.

Responsible AI

AI suggests, contributors decide

Metadata decides whether an image can be found and whether it passes review, so AI suggestions couldn't be silently accepted.

Evidence
Contributors wanted help with repetitive tagging, but content reviewers hold metadata to a quality standard.
Interpretation
Wrong AI metadata would just move the work to quality control, and hurt the contributor.
Decision
AI metadata arrives as suggestions that are easy to see, check and correct before submitting, never as accepted values.
Trade-off
A review step remains. The saving comes from editing instead of typing, not from skipping review.

Validation

Tested with real contributors, not yet measured live

Usability test Three rounds on UserTesting.com with real contributors, who uploaded images, added metadata and used bulk editing. The prototype cut steps from about 25 to about 10, every participant completed their tasks, and satisfaction averaged 4.6 out of 5.

What testing changed

Feedback led me to make bulk editing easier to find and to add a clearer confirmation after saving.

After launchTime on task and quality control time will be measured against the baselines above.

AI only helps when people can see what it did

Transparency is part of the interface. Next, I'm improving how clear AI metadata is and testing complex bulk edit workflows.

Bring contributors in earlier, during ideation, to co-create features that match how they already think and work.

The operational problem

The team curating search had no real control over it

Alamy has a rich library of authentic images, but search results were static. When something wrong or low-quality showed up for customers, the content team had to work through an outdated CRM that was slow, made metadata editing time-consuming, and had no way to change what customers saw quickly. Fixes took days.

What the team needed

  • Faster curation and metadata editing
  • Boosting and suppressing images in search in near real time
  • Less repetitive quality control work
  • A tool that matched their mental model of how search works
The evidence

Shadowing showed where the hours went

I interviewed the content team, watched them work through demos and shadowing, mapped their pain points, and ran weekly feedback sessions throughout design and build.

The decision

Build on the search people already know

Evidence
The team needed to change what customers saw, fast. The old CRM was separate from search, so they couldn't see the effect of their work.
Interpretation
The tool had to match the team's mental model: they think in search results, not database records.
Decision
Design the tool on top of Alamy's existing search interface instead of a separate back-office system, so the team curates in the same view customers see.
Trade-off
Less freedom for a bespoke layout. Internal controls had to fit around the search interface.
Next check
Weekly sessions with the team, to catch where the model broke down.
NeedDesign response
Find the right images fastNew filters and grid views on a faster search interface
Edit metadata without frictionInline editing with authenticity ratings and bulk selection
Organise contentAdd and remove images from collections and lightboxes
Control what customers seeBoost and suppress images in search results, with Undo

I designed the workflow and interactions, and tested prototypes with the content team every week. How boosting and suppression work with the search infrastructure was designed together with search engineers.

The design

One task, end to end: correcting what customers see

A curator spots weak images ranking in a collection and needs them out of customers' results today.

  1. Find them. Filter by collection. Creative and Editorial collections sit in one dropdown, and every selection becomes a removable chip above the grid.
  2. Select and check. Select the images and choose Suppress. Before anything changes, a warning says which are already suppressed and won't be affected.
  3. Confirm, with a way back. A toast confirms how many images changed, with Undo.
  4. Fix the cause. Edit metadata across all selected images at once. Shared values are shown, and fields that differ say "Multiple values".

Why this mattered

Nobody could see the whole business

Alamy had run for years, but no one had an end-to-end view of how it worked, from winning an enterprise customer to onboarding a contributor. Teams worked in silos with fragmented tools and unclear ownership, which led to duplicated work and long turnaround times.

How I did it

Five departments, two journeys, one map each

  1. Interviewed people across Content, Accounts, Marketing, Contracts and Tech
  2. Documented workflows through process walkthroughs
  3. Mapped stakeholders to see dependencies and handoffs
  4. Ran journey mapping workshops to validate and align
  5. Reviewed drafts with every team until they confirmed accuracy

I kept the blueprints detailed enough to surface real insight, but clear enough for non-designers to use.

What I found

The business was duplicating work without knowing it

  • Too many manual steps and outdated communication channels
  • Teams repeated each other's work because processes weren't aligned
  • Unclear ownership and fragmented tools caused long turnaround times
  • No team could see what happened before or after its own stage

How it was used

The brief

A small evening that happens every month

Beyond Zeal curates an intimate live music evening in Trivandrum. Each month there is one artist and one room, and the audience sits close enough to feel every note.

The identity had to feel personal, and it had to come back every month without going stale.

The name

between us

The name describes the experience. The music is shared between the artist and a small audience, rather than performed at a crowd. It is always set in lower case, with "us" in italic, so it reads quietly, like something said rather than announced.

"an evening close enough to remember"

The line on the back of the bookmark and in the closing posts.

Colour system

One constant palette, one colour per edition

Black, off-white and two greys stay the same every month. Each edition adds a single pastel, used for a thin stripe along the top of every piece and as the background of the pastel versions. Every edition looks like part of the same series, while each month still gets its own colour.

Typography

A light serif and a widely spaced sans

Cormorant Garamond in a light weight carries the name and the artist. Raleway, set small in widely spaced capitals, handles every supporting detail. The contrast between the two does the work that decoration usually would.
