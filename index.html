How I work

Start with why

Before designing, I need to know why we're building it and who it helps. On the Lightbox, asking why turned "Lightbox 2.0" into two products, because one tool was serving two very different audiences.

Fight for the experience, with evidence

I don't just draw what's requested. I test, and when two ideas each win somewhere, I combine them. That's how the Search Assistant's placement was decided by users, not by opinion.

Simple over busy

Fewer, clearer choices. Smart Search became a quiet setting so the AI assistant could stand out, and an A/B test confirmed it cost nothing in sales.

Words are design

Two label changes, "Speak your search" and "Classic search", fixed confusion that visual tweaks couldn't. I test copy as seriously as layout.

On-brand, even when AI builds it

Everything I design lives in our design system, tokenised and linked to code. When I use Claude Code and Figma MCP, it builds from that same system, not look-alikes.

Make the invisible visible

Sometimes the most useful design work is a map. Seeing the whole system first is how I find the problems worth solving.

Beyond the screen

Good products come from teams that enjoy working together, so I help bring people together too. Outside work, I'm a trained singer and perform at open mics, which is also great practice for presenting design work to a room.

Why this mattered

Keyword search fails people who know exactly what they want

"A candid woman laughing outdoors in warm golden light" is a sentence, not a list of keywords. People who can describe the image they need often can't turn it into the words search understands, so they give up or settle. The business wanted more search use, more licensing, and a clear AI advantage over competitors.

How do we add a conversational assistant to a familiar search page so people notice it, understand it's different from Smart Search, and can always get back to classic search?

Constraints: results must never be pushed down the page, classic search must keep working for people who don't want AI, and the assistant had to sit beside Smart Search, an existing feature with a very similar name.

What I found

I inherited a concept, and three problems

I took over mid-flight from another designer, and took ownership of research, iteration and the final design. The concept put the chat beside the results, which was a strong idea I kept. But one version was hidden in a filters tab, and chat bubbles, chips and buttons all shared the same green.

The naming was the biggest trap. To users, Smart Search (better keyword matching, a toggle in the search bar) and Search Assistant (a conversation) both sounded like "the AI thing". That gave me three problems to solve in order:

  1. Placement. Where should the assistant live so people notice it without losing their results?
  2. Visibility. How should the entry point look so it reads as something new, not another link?
  3. The clash. How do Smart Search and Search Assistant stop looking like the same thing?
Problem 1: placement

Explore wide, test two opposite bets, then combine

I explored several entry points, from tabs to a sidebar to a button beside filters, then narrowed them to two opposite ideas.

Then I ran three rounds of unmoderated testing on UserTesting.com: 12 participants per round, all regular stock image buyers from the UK, US, Australia, Brazil and India. Every round used the same five tasks, from noticing the feature to getting back to classic search.

MetricA: tabsB: bannerC: hybrid
Noticed without being asked50%75%83%
Time to find (prompted)31.5s18.5s16.5s
Return to classic search (median)54s92s66.5s
Ease inside the assistant (SEQ /7)6.46.26.6

Variant C combined A's clear structure with B's prominence, and named the way back: "← Classic search" instead of an X. It won on discovery and speed. Round 3 confirmed what the final design needed: a prominent entry point, and a named way back.

What shipped: a Search Assistant button on the results page that opens the assistant on its own page, and a "Back to classic search" button inside it.

Try it on alamy.com

Problem 2: visibility

A new AI button, so the assistant never looks like a nav link

Alamy's green already meant "main action": "Learn more", "Create account now", even the old Smart Search. An AI feature in the same green would read as one more of them. So I gave AI its own look: a purple gradient button with a sparkle icon, a Beta tag, and the prompt "Describe the image or video you're looking for."

I added it to Alamy DS 2.0 as a new token family, matched to an "ai" variant of the Button component in code: a pill button, plus 48px (mobile) and 64px (desktop) floating buttons, each with hover and disabled states.

"A new, separate token family, not a reuse of icon/purple, whose defined usage doesn't cover a button surface used repeatedly for a call-to-action."

From my design system notes: why the AI button got its own tokens instead of borrowing an existing colour.

Problem 3: the clash

Redesigning Smart Search so the two features stop competing

A help tooltip explains it in one line: "Try describing what you're looking for in your own words. Switch it off for exact keyword matching."

Validating it with an A/B test

Moving Smart Search was a risk, because it's tied to how people search and buy. So we A/B tested it.

QuestionResult (new toggle vs original)
Did it hurt sales?No evidence. Without a few unusually large orders, revenue , average order value , transactions .
Did search behaviour change?Barely. Image clicks flat at −0.8%.
Is the toggle still easy to find?Less so. "Switch on" interactions fell 18.4%.

The change is commercially safe. The lower discoverability is the trade-off for letting the assistant stand out, and a clear target for the next iteration.

Designing the AI

Designing how the AI behaves, not just where it sits

A conversational tool has states a search box doesn't: an empty conversation, waiting, answers that could be wrong, and people who don't know what to ask. I designed for each one.

MomentRiskWhat I designed
First visitPeople think it generates imagesAn introduction that says what it does and what it doesn't: it finds real Alamy images. Plus pro tips, like "Specify colour, mood and orientation" and "Refine anytime: make it warmer".
Blank startPeople don't know what to askA greeting and "Try asking…" prompts, like "I want blue sky images with bird flying".
Before resultsAn empty page feels brokenA ghost grid that says "Results appear here. Chat with Search Assistant to find the images you need."
Vague requestsToo many irrelevant resultsFollow-up questions that narrow the search, with results updating live beside the chat.
Trusting answersAI suggests images that don't existEvery suggestion links to a real image by its reference number, with a count of matching images.
VoiceNot knowing if the mic is on"Speak your search" with a clear listening state, fixing the top complaint from testing.
Wanting outFeeling trapped in the AI"Back to classic search", always visible at the top of the chat.

What the live beta taught me

The references work: people who find them click them repeatedly. But in the live beta they run inline as raw codes in a dense paragraph, which makes answers hard to scan. The next iteration turns answers into a short intro plus scannable image references, so the answer reads like a designed message, not system output.

One assistant, reachable from anywhere

The assistant has its own page at alamy.com/search-assistant, and the AI button follows people across the site.

What I learned

Four lessons I'll carry forward

Don't pick a winner too early

Rounds 1 and 2 each had a clear strength. Treating them as ingredients showed what the final design needed.

Names are design

"Speak your search" and "Classic search" fixed confusion that visual tweaks couldn't.

AI features need to say what they're not

Several people assumed the assistant generated images. On a marketplace selling real photography, the copy has to be explicit.

Every design choice has a cost, so measure it

The A/B test showed the Smart Search change was safe, and exactly what it cost. Knowing the cost means we can decide, instead of guessing.

What's next

  • Scannable answer formatting for image references
  • Understanding why return to classic search
  • Making the Smart Search toggle easier to find without competing again
  • A feedback widget people actually see

Why this mattered

One feature, doing six different jobs badly

Lightbox was built to let customers save images for later. Over a decade it quietly became the tool behind customer saves, sharing, internal curation, sales outreach, enterprise downloads and even export workflows. It was never designed to carry any of that weight.

What was brokenEvidence
No way to move between collections mid-task. The most common complaint across all 20 sessions.Confirmed by 3 sources
The save icon looked like a navigation icon and never changed state. People couldn't tell if saving had worked.Confirmed by 3 sources
The label field was called "Comment". Three participants expected a caption or title and couldn't tell if they had finished.Usability testing
None of 14 competitors use the word "Lightbox", and every major rival lets people share without logging in. Alamy didn't.Competitive gap, high priority
What I found

Four methods, one validation tracker

I tagged every problem by how many independent sources confirmed it, so design decisions could be prioritised by strength of evidence, not opinion.

MethodWhat it covered
Stakeholder interviews5 internal teams across content operations, curation, distribution and sales, each with its own undocumented workflow built on Lightbox
Usability testing20 participants in 8 countries, 11 tasks each, on the live product rather than a prototype, to get a real baseline
Competitive analysis14 platforms, from Getty Boards to Milanote, scored against the same set of problems
GA4 analyticsA full quarter of live traffic, recovered from raw exports after dashboard widgets hit quota errors

Three findings that reframed the project

<1%GA4, full quarter

Of 6,081 users who interacted with an image inside a Lightbox, only 0.86% downloaded, 0.69% added to cart and 0.51% saved again. Nearly everyone just clicked through to view. This turned "Lightbox feels like a dead end" from an anecdote into the hardest number in the research, and set the bar for what "fixed" had to mean.

32%GA4, page-level data

An internal editorial series, "See The World In 40 Images", drove about 25,750 users, nearly a third of all Lightbox traffic, from just 7 editions. But each edition's page was reused for an unrelated auto-generated page once its run ended. The demand for curated content was real. It just had nowhere permanent to live.

3/3Interview, usability testing and competitive analysis

"No persistent navigation between collections" was the only problem confirmed by all three qualitative methods at once: a stakeholder's own walkthrough, the most common usability complaint, and a named competitive priority.

How I decided

Not "Lightbox 2.0", but two products on one foundation

The research kept pointing to the same root cause. Lightbox served two audiences with very different needs: casual customers saving a handful of images, and professional curators managing briefs of 1,000+ assets. Rebuilding one unified tool would have repeated the original mistake.

Collections Core

The shared foundation: data model, permissions, sharing and restriction handling. Invisible to users, but both products sit on it.

How I planned it

Every step traced to a source

Before drawing screens, I mapped the five core Customer Collections journeys. Each step is annotated with the interview, usability finding, PRD requirement or analytics number that justifies it. Nothing is in the flow because it seemed reasonable.

  1. First saveOutcome: saving never silently fails
  2. Save to an existing collectionOutcome: organise by project without losing context
  3. Manage a collectionOutcome: saved assets stay findable and manageable over time
  4. Share a collectionOutcome: shared collections are clear, secure and usable
  5. Enterprise downloadOutcome: eligible assets download reliably
What I built

Eight screens, one evidenced flow

The wireframes follow the journey map directly: no screen exists that isn't a step in the map. Two gaps only appeared once wireframing was underway: a true empty-collection state, and a way to add assets from inside a collection.

From wireframes to design

On-brand, in the real design system

I designed all eight screens in Alamy's real design system: the brand green, black navigation and real components. A teammate had built a working prototype in the same tokens, and for one screen I adopted its paste-by-reference pattern because it matched how the team actually works.

How I checked it

Auditing the design against the PRD, line by line

Once the wireframes existed, I checked every one of the PRD's 33 customer-facing requirements against the journey map, rather than assuming they were covered. I ran this audit, and the evidence tracking behind it, with Claude Code, then reviewed every finding myself.

Where it stands

Design in progress, with a baseline to beat

Discovery and journey mapping are complete, and all eight Customer Collections screens exist as wireframes and on-brand designs. Naming, migration strategy and a few other product decisions are open with the PM before handoff.

How I'll know it worked

Because testing ran on the live product and analytics covered a full quarter, every measure has a real baseline:

  • Downloads and add-to-cart from inside a collection, starting from under 1%
  • Ease of reordering, the lowest-scoring task in testing at 4.6/5
  • Ease of multi-select and move, at 4.75/5 on the current Lightbox
  • A permanent home for curated content like "See The World In 40 Images"

What I'd take from this

The most useful discipline wasn't any single research method. It was refusing to let a design decision stand without a named source behind it. That is what surfaced CC-001 and the video inconsistency: not new research, just a systematic check against what had already been decided.

Why I started over

Not a copy of Customer Collections

After finishing Customer Collections, it was tempting to reuse its journey structure. I didn't. Curators are a different group, doing different work at a much larger scale, and forcing one shape onto both would hide what makes this tool different.

So I started from the evidence:

  1. Raw evidence first, from the pain-point map
  2. Then the PRD for Curated Collections
  3. Then a stress test: do the PRD's four journeys actually cover what the evidence points to?

They mostly did. But three themes, export and API access, real-time collaboration, and territory-scoped views, had zero coverage across all 40 requirements.

Two different interviewees raised them independently, with a concrete solution already on record.

What I mapped

Four flows, evidence first, gaps included

I used the same approach as Customer Collections: every step traced to a source. But this map also says openly when a journey rests on requirements rather than a validated complaint.

  1. Build a collectionSearch, select and create
  2. RefineReorder, edit details and metadata
  3. Publish and shareMake a collection public and share its URL
  4. Unpublish or deleteFlagged as requirement-led: no interview asked for it, but a publish lifecycle needs an end state
How I decided

Going back to the tool I'd already designed

Seven wireframes in, working from the PRD alone, I went back to the Curation Tool file, the tool I designed in 2024 for the content team. Curated collection screens had already been started there in Alamy Design System 2.0, some marked "Ready for dev", and my new wireframes didn't match them.

I had two honest options: keep my new version, or throw out the guesswork and rebuild to match. I rebuilt, working from the real components in Alamy DS 2.0 through Figma MCP, so the new screens use the same system as the live tool. Three of my assumptions turned out to be wrong in ways that mattered:

What I assumedWhat the real tool does
ThemeA light theme, matching Customer CollectionsDark, a deliberate choice for an internal, image-heavy curation tool
StructureCollection management in modalsA persistent right-side panel, as a third tab next to Metadata and Boost images, tools curators already use every day
List viewA card grid, copied from Customer CollectionsA dense, sortable table, right for people managing hundreds of collections
What I built

Seven screens, matched to the real system

Every screen follows the real Curation Tool's patterns where I could check them. Where I couldn't, the screen says so.

Where it stands

Six screens verified, three gaps handed to the PM

Six of seven wireframes are confirmed against the real Curation Tool. Three evidenced gaps, export and API access, live collaboration, and a lower-priority "add by reference ID" workflow, are flagged for the PM rather than designed around on my own.

What I'd take from this

The useful instinct wasn't building fast. It was being willing to throw out seven screens the moment better information appeared, instead of quietly keeping the version I'd already invested in. The rebuilt wireframes are more accurate because the first version was wrong in specific, nameable ways.

Why this mattered

The team curating search had no real control over it

Alamy has a rich library of authentic images, but search results were static. The content team worked in an outdated CRM that was slow and limited, made metadata editing time-consuming, and had no way to influence what customers saw in search quickly.

Goals

  • Speed up curation and metadata editing
  • Let the team boost and suppress images in search in near real time
  • Automate repetitive quality control tasks
  • Match the team's mental model of how search works
What I found

Shadowing showed where the hours went

I interviewed the content team, watched them work through demos and shadowing, mapped their pain points, and ran weekly feedback sessions throughout design and build.

  • The old CRM was slow and severely limited
  • Editing metadata took multiple steps
  • Quality control was repetitive, with no automation
  • The team needed to change search results, and needed it fast
How I decided

Build on the search people already know

Instead of a separate back-office system, I designed the tool on top of Alamy's existing search interface. The team could curate in the same view customers see, which made the effect of every change easy to understand.

NeedDesign response
Find the right images fastNew filters and grid views on a faster search interface
Edit metadata without frictionInline editing with authenticity ratings and bulk selection
Organise contentAdd and remove images from collections and lightboxes
Control what customers seeBoost and suppress images in search results

I tested prototypes with the content team every week, and partnered with search engineers to make boosting and suppression work with the search infrastructure.

What I learned

Internal users shape what customers see

Improving the content team's tools directly improved the relevance of search for customers. Internal users deserve the same care as external ones.

A tool that keeps growing

Because the tool was built on a solid structure, it became the home for new work. In 2026, Curated Collections joined it as a third tab beside Metadata and Boost images, so curators build collections in the tool they already use every day.

What I'd do differently

Shadow the team for longer, to catch the edge cases that only appear after weeks of daily use.

Why this mattered

Nobody could see the whole business

Alamy had run for years, but no one had an end-to-end view of how it worked, from winning an enterprise customer to onboarding a contributor. Teams worked in silos with fragmented tools and unclear ownership, which led to duplicated work and long turnaround times.

How I did it

Five departments, two journeys, one map each

  1. Interviewed people across Content, Accounts, Marketing, Contracts and Tech
  2. Documented workflows through process walkthroughs
  3. Mapped stakeholders to see dependencies and handoffs
  4. Ran journey mapping workshops to validate and align
  5. Reviewed drafts with every team until they confirmed accuracy

I kept the blueprints detailed enough to surface real insight, but clear enough for non-designers to use.

What I found

The business was duplicating work without knowing it

  • Too many manual steps and outdated communication channels
  • Teams repeated each other's work because processes weren't aligned
  • Unclear ownership and fragmented tools caused long turnaround times
  • No team could see what happened before or after its own stage

Impact

From a map to a redesign

I presented the blueprints to leadership and cross-functional teams. They became a single source of truth for how processes worked and showed leadership bottlenecks they hadn't known about.

"This is the first time we've been able to see the full picture. It's amazing how much we were duplicating work without realizing it."

The contributor blueprint became the starting point for the Contributor Experience Revamp I'm leading in 2025.

What I learned

Sometimes the design work is making the invisible visible

Mapping internal and external experiences together built a shared understanding that guided later product work.

What I'd do differently

Make the blueprints a living document teams could update as processes change, so they stay useful over time.
