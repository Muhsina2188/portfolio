The problem

Search beyond keywords, without breaking search

Alamy's customers found images through keyword search, with Smart Search to improve their queries. There was no way to describe the image you wanted in your own words, or by voice. "A candid woman laughing outdoors in warm golden light" is a sentence, not a list of keywords.

The job wasn't just to add a chatbot. It was to design a genuinely useful conversational search, and fit it into a website with established search behaviours, controls and commercial priorities.

How do we give Search Assistant a distinct role alongside keyword search and Smart Search, without forcing anyone away from a familiar workflow?

Research

Starting with the problem, not the chatbot

Problem

Semantic search improved result quality, but people still had to squeeze project needs into a search box, with no way to explore, refine and discuss a brief.

Hypothesis

Conversational search would let people discuss briefs, refine ideas and see results inline, turning search into a collaborative assistant.

User goal

Find the right images or videos quickly and confidently through conversation, without having to search, filter or contact support.

Business goal

Help customers discover and buy through conversation, know this kind of search exists, and rely less on support.

One brief, five competitors

I gave the same long, natural-language brief to five competitors: two people in their 50s walking hand in hand along a riverside in spring, in the 1940s, with period clothing, dilapidated buildings and horses in the field.

GettyYesPartly: the age, not the 1940sNo
iStockYesPartly: the age, not the 1940sNo
UnsplashYesNo: unrelated imagesNo
EnvatoNo, a word limit cut it short——

Even where competitors accepted a conversational request, nothing told people they could search that way. So the Assistant had to announce what it could do.

Fitting it into search

From "people can't find it" to a distinct entry point

I tested the entry point in UserTesting.com sessions, asking participants to find the Assistant, describe the experience and rate its ease of use.

Usability test People initially struggled to notice the Assistant, and to tell it apart from Smart Search.

Placement concepts 6+ concepts explored

The decision chain

  1. Testing

    People overlooked the Assistant or confused it with Smart Search.

  2. Insight

    Not a position problem: a hierarchy and identity problem. Both features looked like the same kind of thing.

  3. Design

    A distinct row with its own AI button, and Smart Search simplified into a compact toggle.

  4. A/B evidence

    Switch-on interactions fell while image clicks remained broadly stable; the test showed no clear negative commercial impact.

  5. Final entry point

    Released, with Smart Search's lower discoverability kept as an open question.

The A/B test compared these two Smart Search designs only. It is not evidence that Search Assistant increased conversion, revenue or adoption.

The released entry point

Alamy's green already meant "main action", and the design system's only purple was an icon token. So I created a dedicated AI button with its own colour tokens, pill and floating versions, and hover and disabled states. I built it in code, handed the code to engineering, and added it to the design system as a new style of the existing Button, so future AI features can reuse it.

Opening the Assistant doesn't mean starting over: it carries an earlier keyword search into the conversation and shows matching results straight away.

Team and process

What was mine, and how I worked

  • Mine: the experience on desktop and mobile, its integration into Alamy.com, the placement exploration, the Smart Search simplification, the AI button (designed, coded and added to the design system), and usability testing.
  • With the PM: releasing as a beta, and iterating from customer feedback.
  • With engineering: they built from my Figma designs and coded prototypes, and integrated the AI button code I gave them.
  • With analytics: the Smart Search A/B test and beta tracking.

Instead of step-by-step Figma click-throughs, I built coded, production-ready prototypes with Claude Code and Alamy's code-connected design system. Stakeholders reviewed the real behaviour; I deployed them to Vercel to test in UserTesting.com sessions earlier and more realistically; and engineers used them alongside the Figma designs, which remain the place for ideation and the single source of truth.

Beta and next

Release when it's good to go, then keep improving

Search Assistant went live as a beta in September 2026, less than four months after the project began. The approach is to release when the experience is ready, learn from real customer feedback, and evolve it into the full version.

Next: shorter answers with a few best-fit picks

Beta answers run as long paragraphs with raw reference codes, which makes them hard to scan. One direction being explored: a one-line summary, three best-fit images with a short reason each, and suggested refinements.

Evidence

Three sources, three different questions

Each source answers its own question. None of them is combined into a single impact score.

Usability test

Can people find it, and tell it apart?

Found: people overlooked the Assistant and confused it with Smart Search, which led to the change in hierarchy and identity.

Doesn't show: that the released entry point improves discovery in live use.

A/B test

Is simplifying Smart Search safe?

  • −18.4% switch-on interactions
  • −0.8% search-result image clicks
  • No clear negative commercial impact; revenue was sensitive to a few high-value orders

Doesn't show: anything about Search Assistant's adoption or impact. It tested the Smart Search redesign only.

Beta survey

How easy does the beta feel?

  • 6.7/10 average ease of use
  • 57% rated it 8–10
  • 23 responses

Liked: speed, and handling specific requests. Fell short: irrelevant results, exclusions ("without…"), image zoom and resolution.

Doesn't show: satisfaction across all customers, or business impact. It's a small, early sample.

Open question Search Assistant's own adoption, engagement and commercial impact, to be added once verified and approved.

What I learned

Three lessons I'll carry forward

  • Design the experience, then earn its place. A good conversational experience still fails if people can't find it, or can't tell it apart from what's already there.
  • Separation isn't identity. Moving the Assistant away from Smart Search didn't make it noticeable. A clear identity and place in the hierarchy did.
  • Validate changes to what already works. Smart Search contributed to conversions, so simplifying it was tested before it was confirmed.

The problem and stakes

One feature, doing six different jobs

Should one overloaded feature keep serving customers and internal curators, or do these audiences need distinct experiences?

Lightbox was built to let customers save images for later. Over a decade it quietly became the tool behind customer saves, sharing, internal curation, sales outreach, enterprise downloads and even export workflows. It was never designed to carry that weight, and the brief I was given was simply to rebuild it.

The evidence

Four sources, each with a blind spot

No single method could answer the question, so I combined four and was explicit about what each could and couldn't tell me.

SourceWhat it could tell meWhat it couldn't
Stakeholder interviews
5 internal teams		How content, curation, distribution and sales each depend on Lightbox, often through undocumented workflowsHow customers behave
Usability testing
20 participants, live product		Where people struggle today, giving a real baselineHow often those problems happen at scale
Competitive review
14 platforms		What customers expect from similar tools, and where Alamy falls behindWhether Alamy's customers want the same things
Product analytics
A full quarter		What people actually do inside Lightbox, at scaleWhy they do it

How I judged confidence

I tagged every problem by how many independent sources confirmed it, so decisions could be prioritised by strength of evidence rather than by who said it loudest.

ProblemConfidence
No way to move between collections mid-task. The most common complaint in testing.3 sources

The insight

The symptoms pointed to one cause

Dead endAnalytics

Almost everyone who opened an image inside a Lightbox only viewed it. Very few downloaded, added to cart or saved again. That turned "Lightbox feels like a dead end" from an anecdote into evidence, and set the bar for what "fixed" had to mean.

CurationAnalytics

An internal editorial series, "See The World In 40 Images", drew a large share of all Lightbox traffic from just a handful of editions. But each edition's page was reused once its run ended. The demand for curated content was real. It had nowhere permanent to live.

3/3Interviews, testing, competitive review

"No persistent navigation between collections" was the only problem confirmed by all three qualitative methods at once.

Together these showed that the usability problems were symptoms. The cause was one feature serving two audiences with very different needs: customers saving a handful of images, and professional curators managing briefs of 1,000+ assets.

The decision

Not "Lightbox 2.0", but two experiences on one foundation

Evidence
Customers and curators work at different scales with different goals, and five internal teams had built their own workflows on top of the same feature.
Interpretation
Rebuilding one unified tool would repeat the original mistake.
Decision
Separate Customer Collections and Curated Collections, sharing one foundation for data, permissions, sharing and restrictions.
Trade-off
Two experiences to design and maintain, and a migration question for existing Lightboxes, now open with the PM.

Product context

A feature inside a tool curators already use

The Curation Tool is the internal product the content team uses to improve search results and collections for customers. Curated Collections adds a third tab beside Metadata and Boost images, so curators build collections in the place where they already work.

How we got here

One overloaded feature, split in two

  1. The brief

    "Rebuild Lightbox." But over a decade it had quietly taken on six jobs, from customer saves to internal curation and sales outreach.

  2. The research

    Interviews, usability testing, competitors and analytics pointed to one cause: customers saving a handful of images and curators managing 1,000+ were sharing one tool.

  3. The decision

    Split it. Curated Collections for internal users inside the Curation Tool, and My Collection for customers, on one shared foundation.

Read the full reasoning in My Collection →

The problem

Internal curation had been living in a customer feature

Lightbox was built for customers to save images. Over the years, internal teams adopted it for curation, sales outreach and editorial series, because nothing else let them build and share a set of images.

  • Research finding Curators work at a very different scale from customers, managing briefs of 1,000+ assets, in a tool designed for saving a handful of images.
  • Research finding An internal editorial series drew a large share of all Lightbox traffic, but each edition's page was reused once its run ended. The demand for curated content was real; it had nowhere permanent to live.

How might curators build, organise and share collections at scale, inside the tool they already use, without borrowing a customer feature?

Discovery

Starting from research, not the requirements document

It was tempting to reuse the customer journey from the Lightbox rebuild. I didn't: curators do different work at a much larger scale. I started from the raw research, including stakeholder interviews across content operations, curation, distribution and sales, and then stress-tested all 40 requirements against it.

The requirements mostly held up. But three themes had no coverage at all: export and API access, real-time collaboration, and territory-scoped views. Two interviewees raised them independently.

I flagged the three gaps to the PM instead of designing around them on my own.

The operational problem

The team curating search had no real control over it

Alamy has a rich library of authentic images, but search results were static. When something wrong or low-quality showed up for customers, the content team had to work through an outdated CRM that was slow, made metadata editing time-consuming, and had no way to change what customers saw quickly. Fixes took days.

What the team needed

  • Faster curation and metadata editing
  • Boosting and suppressing images in search in near real time
  • Less repetitive quality control work
  • A tool that matched their mental model of how search works
The evidence

Shadowing showed where the hours went

I interviewed the content team, watched them work through demos and shadowing, mapped their pain points, and ran weekly feedback sessions throughout design and build.

The decision

Build on the search people already know

Evidence
The team needed to change what customers saw, fast. The old CRM was separate from search, so they couldn't see the effect of their work.
Interpretation
The tool had to match the team's mental model: they think in search results, not database records.
Decision
Design the tool on top of Alamy's existing search interface instead of a separate back-office system, so the team curates in the same view customers see.
Trade-off
Less freedom for a bespoke layout. Internal controls had to fit around the search interface.

I designed the workflow and interactions, and tested prototypes with the content team every week. How boosting and suppression work with the search infrastructure was designed together with search engineers.

The design

One task, end to end: correcting what customers see

A curator spots weak images ranking in a collection and needs them out of customers' results today.

  1. Find them. Filter by collection. Creative and Editorial collections sit in one dropdown, and every selection becomes a removable chip above the grid.
  2. Select and check. Select the images and choose Suppress. Before anything changes, a warning says which are already suppressed and won't be affected.
  3. Confirm, with a way back. A toast confirms how many images changed, with Undo.
  4. Fix the cause. Edit metadata across all selected images at once. Shared values are shown, and fields that differ say "Multiple values".

What it revealed

The business was running on email and handoffs

  • Email and meetings carried almost every step. Outlook and Teams appear across all three enterprise journeys.
  • Simple outcomes needed long chains. Creating one iQ account passed from an account development manager to IT to a QA team and back.
  • Manual work where automation would help: monthly invoices, contract mark-ups, and quality checks done by hand in the CRM.
  • Contributor tooling depended on legacy technology, such as an Internet Explorer plug-in for quality control.
  • No team could see what happened before or after its own stage.

I presented the blueprints to leadership and cross-functional teams. They became a shared reference for how processes worked and showed leadership bottlenecks they hadn't known about.

"This is the first time we've been able to see the full picture. It's amazing how much we were duplicating work without realizing it."
What I learned

Sometimes the design work is making the invisible visible

Mapping what customers see together with the work behind it built a shared understanding that guided later product work.

What I'd do differently

Make the blueprints a living document teams could update as processes change, so they stay useful over time.

The brief

A small evening that happens every month

Beyond Zeal curates an intimate live music evening in Trivandrum. Each month there is one artist and one room, and the audience sits close enough to feel every note.

The identity had to feel personal, and it had to come back every month without going stale.

The name

between us

The name describes the experience. The music is shared between the artist and a small audience, rather than performed at a crowd. It is always set in lower case, with "us" in italic, so it reads quietly, like something said rather than announced.

"an evening close enough to remember"

The line on the back of the bookmark and in the closing posts.

Colour system

One constant palette, one colour per edition

Black, off-white and two greys stay the same every month. Each edition adds a single pastel, used for a thin stripe along the top of every piece and as the background of the pastel versions. Every edition looks like part of the same series, while each month still gets its own colour.
